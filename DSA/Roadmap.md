# My DSA Journey: How I Grew a Backpacker's Backend Background into Big Tech Readiness

Look, let's be real. Coming from a solid web development and backend background, I knew how to build things, but writing raw algorithms from scratch wasn't my daily bread. I knew the basics, but if I actually wanted to crack Big Tech, I had to stop messing around and get serious.

Most people start from the comfortable side—easy arrays and strings—and burn out when things get hard. I decided to flip the script and go straight for the throat by tackling the hardest stuff first.

## The Reverse-Order Roadmap

I figured that if I started with the nightmare stuff, everything else would feel like a walk in the park. And honestly? It worked.

Here is the exact order I took:

1. Dynamic Programming (Started right here at the absolute peak of difficulty)
2. Graphs
3. Trees
4. Backtracking
5. Linked Lists
6. Stacks
7. Binary Search
8. Sliding Window
9. Greedy
10. Bit Manipulation

**What I Skipped:** I completely skipped Heaps as a standalone module because you end up touching them naturally in things like Dijkstra and graph algorithms anyway. I also skipped Intervals. Don't waste time on stuff that absorbs organically elsewhere.

## The Grind: 200 Hours to Full Coverage

I wasn't casually scrolling through problems. I was putting in 6 hours every single day—high-concentration, deep-focus hours where I tried to maximize every minute.

**The Timeline:**

- Dynamic Programming kicked my ass for about 8 to 9 days straight.
- Graphs took roughly a week.
- But after breaking through those two, every single topic required less and less time. By the time I hit Stacks, Binary Search, and Sliding Window, I was clearing them in max 2 days each.

**The Goal:** Total coverage. I wanted to build a mental map so robust that I could look at almost any problem and know what it was asking for.

In total, it took me around 200 hours to get that full coverage across the board. The return on investment felt insane because once you tackle the worst parts, the rest is just pattern matching.

**A note on how this phase actually feels:** at the start, it's insanely hard and exhausting—but it's also insanely exciting. Every single day you feel real progress, because you're walking through the roadmap and constantly running into something you've never seen before. Don't be scared to forget things along the way; the point of this phase isn't to retain everything perfectly, it's that you're actually experiencing it. That exposure is what matters. This is the fun part, and it stays fun the whole way through—because you're always learning something new.

## Leveling Up: Pattern Recognition & Hard Tasks (~300 Hours Total)

Here's the part nobody warns you about: once you've built that coverage, that's exactly when it stops being fun. Your motivation will drop hard, and discipline is the only thing that gets you through it. The reason is simple — you're no longer learning a stream of new things every day. Now you're just drilling how to apply what you already know to real problems, over and over. For me, even considering myself a pretty disciplined person, this stretch was brutally hard to push through. I ended up cutting my daily grind from 6 hours down to 3 just to keep showing up. The only thing that actually matters here is consistency—putting in the time every single day, even when it feels repetitive.

Once coverage was done, I made a call: mediums weren't useless, but grinding them wasn't going to build the specific ability I was after. I wanted to be able to sit down and solve hards, full stop—no mediums as a warm-up, no easing into it. So I decided to go all-in on hards and only hards.

So, I changed how I practiced. I started using AI—strictly as a problem generator with zero hints allowed—to feed me curated lists of hard problems. I also pulled hard problems from HackerRank to keep the pool from running dry.

**My rules were strict:**

- Hards: 30 minutes max.
- Time Complexity First: Before writing a single line of code, I forced myself to brutally break down time and space complexity.

By the time I hit around 300 hours total, something clicked. I stopped seeing random coding puzzles and started instantly recognizing core patterns. Under pressure, I could break down hard tasks without panicking. When I hit mock interviews after this routine, I was consistently scoring 8.5 out of 10.

If you're jumping into this repo, use it as a blueprint for how to actually attack the problem head-on instead of wasting time the easy way.

## How the Repository is Structured

If you're wondering how to actually navigate through this, I didn't just dump everything into one giant file. 

Inside each specific domain folder (for example, `/dynamic-programming` or `/graphs`), you’ll find:
* **The Topic Roadmap:** A deeper dive into the specific mental models and breakdowns for that category.
* **The Tasks Tracker:** A dedicated task file/table containing the exact problems I tackled, complete with direct links, status tracking, definitions, and notes. 

Feel free to grab the list, check out the problems, and test yourself against the same progression.
